This is a much stronger document. The question is now sharp, the certificate is the methodological core, and section 10 preempts the obvious objection. It reads like a paper with a thesis rather than a dataset with a wrapper. Second-pass issues, roughly in order of importance:

**1. Demanding a certificate is itself a hint.** If the prompt says "produce an invariant/monovariant/coloring that verifies," you've told the model the idea class, which is hint level 3 in your own hierarchy. You need two conditions: *free solve*, where the model works unprompted and the certificate is either extracted from the trace or requested afterwards, and *certificate-demanded*. The gap between them is interesting in its own right (it's essentially H₅₀ measured differently), but reporting only the demanded condition would overstate idea discovery. The draft dropped the earlier "idea first, then solve" protocol without saying what replaced it — make this explicit.

**2. Certificates confound idea-having with idea-formalizing.** A model may see the invariant and fail to write it as a checkable function. Add a formalization-only baseline: give the idea in prose, ask for the certificate. If a model can't formalize ideas it's handed, its I=0 scores mean something different.

**3. Guard against degenerate and searched certificates.** A constant function is an invariant. The certificate needs to be verified *and* discriminating (separates initial from target, or bounds the answer) — you say this for parity; make it a general requirement. More fundamentally: a model with code execution can enumerate candidate invariants and test them, which is idea discovery *by search*. The search/insight dichotomy isn't disjoint, and reviewers will say so. Section 11 already gestures at "search converting into structural discovery"; I'd make that the explicit framing: instance-level search vs. idea-level search vs. immediate recognition, with tool access as a controlled variable. That's more defensible and more interesting than the binary.

**4. The predicted curve separation is close to guaranteed; say so.** S(n | I=0) falling and S(n | I=1) staying flat is nearly forced by construction once brute force is infeasible. That's a validity check on the certificate, not the finding. The finding is I(n) itself, I(d), and I(c). Reframe section 6 accordingly, or a reviewer will call the headline prediction trivial. Also note that conditioning on I=1 shifts the population as n grows, so keep enough samples per cell and consider the unconditioned S(n) alongside.

**5. Verification at large n needs a plan.** Exhaustive checking over the state space is only feasible at small n. Say what you do above that: verify exhaustively on small members of the family, sample transitions at large n, or accept symbolic certificates. Otherwise "mechanically verified" quietly stops being true at exactly the sizes the scaling experiment cares about.

**6. Some families collapse idea and algorithm.** In Nim, computing the XOR *is* the solution — there's no execution left after the idea. Those families won't show a search regime at all. Separate families by whether the idea yields an O(1)-style proof or an efficient algorithm; the scaling signature differs.

**7. Scope.** Five experiments (scaling, transfer, compute, latency, hints) plus three artifacts is a lot. The minimum paper is: ~8 families with clean certificates, the scaling and transfer experiments across 4–5 models, and the compute sweep on two of them. Hints and latency can be appendix or a second paper. Given how you like to work, I'd build that minimal version first and see whether the I(n) and I(d) curves say anything surprising before investing in the rest.

**8. Small things.** State the tool setting (no code execution in the main results, or the whole search-infeasibility argument weakens). Say who generates Novel and Transfer instances; if it's an LLM, note the risk that they're biased toward what LLMs find natural. The title's "Mechanically Verifiable Study" is awkward — "Verifying Idea Discovery in Language Models" or similar.
